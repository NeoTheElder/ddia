The Trouble with Distributed Systems

Hey I just met you
The network's laggy
But here's my data
So store it maybe — Kyle Kingsbury, Carly Rae Jepsen and the Perils of Network Partitions (2013)

The last few chapters kept meeting things going wrong — failover (Chapter 5←5), replication lag, isolation anomalies (Chapter 7←7) — and were still, the book says, too optimistic. This chapter turns the pessimism all the way up: anything that can go wrong will go wrong (with one exception — nodes are assumed not to lie; see Byzantine faults). Writing software for several computers connected by a network is fundamentally different from writing for one, and the difference is the sheer number of new ways to fail. The job is still to build systems that meet their guarantees anyway; Chapter 9→9 has the algorithms, but first you have to know the enemy.

Three enemies, in order: networks that lose and delay packets without telling you; clocks that drift, jump, and disagree — plus processes that stop running for minutes without noticing; and then the disorienting consequence — that a node cannot know anything for sure, only guess from the messages it does or doesn't receive. The chapter ends by making that philosophical fog precise: system models, safety and liveness.

FEYNMAN · the whole chapter in one idea On one computer, a program either works or it crashes; the hardware hides physics behind a mathematically perfect model. Spread the program over a network and the physics leaks back in. Every conversation between two machines is two people in separate rooms passing notes under a door that sometimes eats them. If you slide a note and nothing comes back, you can't tell whether your note was eaten, the other person left, their reply was eaten, or they're just slow — those four cases look identical from your side. Each room also has its own wall clock, and the clocks disagree by an unknown amount. And any person can fall asleep for a minute mid-sentence and wake up believing no time has passed. The whole chapter is the consequences of those three facts, and its one constructive move is: never let a single person decide anything important — take a vote. Colors as always: ember = writes / requests sent, cyan = reads / responses.
faults-and-partial-failures

Faults and Partial Failures

Software on a single computer is predictable: it works or it doesn't. When hardware fails, the usual consequence is total failure — kernel panic, blue screen — and that is a deliberate design choice: a computer would rather crash than return a wrong answer, because wrong answers are confusing to deal with. So computers hide the fuzzy physical reality underneath and present an idealized model of perfect computation. A CPU instruction always does the same thing; memory doesn't randomly corrupt. That goal goes back to the first digital computer.

Across a network, you lose that idealization and must confront the physical world — where, in Coda Hale's list, things that have gone wrong include long-lived partitions inside one datacenter, PDU failures, switch failures, whole-rack power cycles, whole-DC backbone and power failures, and a hypoglycemic driver crashing a pickup into a datacenter's air conditioning. The defining phenomenon is partial failure: some parts of the system are broken in unpredictable ways while others work fine. Partial failures are nondeterministic — the same operation across nodes may work or unpredictably fail — and, worse, you may not even know whether it succeeded, because message delay is nondeterministic too. That nondeterminism is what makes distributed systems hard.

Cloud Computing and Supercomputing

cloud-computing-and-supercomputing

There is a spectrum of philosophies for large-scale computing. At one end, high-performance computing: supercomputers with thousands of CPUs for weather forecasting or molecular dynamics. At the other, cloud computing, vaguely defined but associated with multi-tenant datacenters, commodity machines on IP/Ethernet, elastic allocation, and metered billing. Enterprise datacenters sit between. The two ends handle faults in opposite ways. A supercomputer job checkpoints to durable storage; when a node fails, the whole cluster workload is stopped, the node repaired, and the job restarted from the last checkpoint. That makes a supercomputer more like a single big computer: partial failure is escalated into total failure, like a kernel panic. Internet services can't do that, for reasons that fill a table:

Hence the stance of everything that follows: accept partial failure and build fault tolerance into the software — a reliable system from unreliable components. Even small systems must think this way: sooner or later some part fails, and the fault handling must be designed in and understood by operators. Don't assume faults are rare; consider a wide range, including unlikely ones, and deliberately create them in testing. In the book's words: suspicion, pessimism, and paranoia pay off.

FEYNMAN · building reliable systems from unreliable parts It sounds like a system can be no stronger than its weakest link. It's an old idea (von Neumann, 1956) that this is false. Error-correcting codes push clean data across a channel that flips bits. IP drops, delays, duplicates, and reorders packets; TCP on top of it retransmits, deduplicates, and reorders back — a reliable stream from an unreliable one. But there is always a ceiling: a code can fix a few bit errors, not a signal drowned in noise (Shannon's limit), and TCP can hide loss but cannot remove delay. The higher layer is not perfect; it's useful because the faults it leaves behind are easier to reason about. The book returns to this as the end-to-end argument in Chapter 12→12.

Unreliable Networks

The systems in this book are shared-nothing: machines with their own memory and disk, connected only by a network — cheap, commodity-friendly, and able to be redundant across geographically distributed datacenters. The internet and most datacenter networks (Ethernet) are asynchronous packet networks: a node can send a packet, but the network promises nothing about when it arrives or whether it arrives. Send a request and expect a response, and six different things can go wrong — the request is lost, the request is queued, the remote node crashed, the remote node is paused (a long GC), the response is lost, the response is queued. From the sender's seat these are indistinguishable: the only fact you have is "no response yet." The usual remedy is a timeout — give up after a while — but a timeout tells you nothing about whether the remote side got the request, and a queued request may still be delivered after you've given up.

Network Faults in Practice

network-faults-in-practice

Decades of networking have not produced reliable networks. One study of a medium-sized datacenter found about 12 network faults per month, half disconnecting a single machine and half a whole rack. Another measured top-of-rack switches, aggregation switches, and load balancers, and found that redundant gear helps less than hoped because it doesn't guard against human error — misconfigured switches are a major cause of outages. EC2 is notorious for transient glitches; well-run private networks are calmer but nobody is immune: a switch software upgrade triggered a topology reconfiguration that delayed packets for more than a minute; sharks bite undersea cables; a network interface was seen dropping all inbound packets while sending outbound ones fine — a link working in one direction says nothing about the other.

Terminology: a part of the network cut off from the rest is a network partition or netsplit; the book prefers network fault to avoid collision with storage partitions (Chapter 6←6). Even if faults are rare for you, the fact that they can happen means the software must handle them — and untested handling does arbitrary things: clusters that deadlock and never recover even after the network heals; systems that delete all your data. Handling doesn't have to mean tolerating: showing users an error during an outage can be a valid design. But you must know how your software reacts and that it can recover, which argues for deliberately inducing network faults (Chaos Monkey, Chapter 1←1).

Detecting Faults

detecting-faults

Many systems must detect dead nodes automatically: a load balancer must take a dead backend out of rotation; a single-leader database must promote a follower when the leader dies. Network uncertainty makes this hard. Sometimes you get explicit feedback: if the machine is reachable but no process listens on the port, the OS replies with a TCP RST/FIN (though if the node crashed mid-request, you don't know how much it processed); if the process died but the OS lives, a script can tell peers immediately (HBase does this); with access to switch management interfaces you can detect link failures at the hardware level (not on the internet or in a shared datacenter); a router may send ICMP Destination Unreachable — but routers have no magic failure detector either. Rapid feedback is useful but can't be counted on. Even a TCP ack proves only that the packet arrived, not that the application handled it before crashing; only a positive response from the application itself means success. In general you must assume no response at all: retry a few times, wait for a timeout, and then declare the node dead.

Timeouts and Unbounded Delays

timeouts-and-unbounded-delays

If a timeout is the only sure detector, how long should it be? There's no simple answer. Long: users wait or see errors while a dead node is still trusted. Short: faults are caught fast, but a node that merely hiccuped (load spike, network burst) is declared dead prematurely. That is dangerous twice over. If the node was mid-action — sending an email — and another node takes over, the action happens twice. And a "dead" node's responsibilities move to other nodes, adding load to them and to the network; if the system was already struggling, the false death sentence can trigger a cascading failure where, in the extreme, every node declares every other dead and everything stops (the same spiral seen with automatic rebalancing in Chapter 6).

In a fictitious network with a maximum packet delay d and nodes that always respond within r, every successful request would complete within 2d + r, and that would be a correct timeout. Real systems have neither bound: asynchronous networks have unbounded delays, and most servers cannot guarantee a maximum response time. Fast most of the time isn't enough — a brief spike in round-trip times knocks a low timeout off balance.

Network congestion and queueing

Like road travel, packet delay varies mostly because of queueing, and it queues in several places: at a switch, when several senders target the same link the packets wait in the outbound queue — and if it fills, packets are dropped and must be resent although nothing is broken (network congestion); at the receiving OS, when all cores are busy the request sits until the application is free; in virtualized hosts, a VM is paused tens of milliseconds while another uses the core and its inbound data is buffered by the hypervisor; and at the sender, where TCP's flow control (congestion avoidance, backpressure) throttles transmission before data even enters the network. On top of that, TCP treats an unacknowledged packet as lost after a timeout derived from observed round-trips and retransmits — the application never sees the loss, only the delay.

FEYNMAN · TCP versus UDP, and where the retry lives Videoconferencing and VoIP use UDP precisely because it doesn't do what TCP does: no flow control, no retransmission, so fewer sources of variable delay (it still suffers switch queues and scheduling). That's the right trade when late data is worthless — a lost audio packet can't be retransmitted before its slot is due to play, so the app inserts a moment of silence and moves on. The retry moves up to the human layer: "sorry, you cut out — could you repeat that?" Reliability and delay-variability are a dial, not a free lunch.

In public clouds and multi-tenant datacenters — where links, switches, NICs, and even CPUs are shared, and a MapReduce job next door (Chapter 10→10) can saturate a link — delays are highly variable and you can't see why. So timeouts can only be chosen experimentally: measure the round-trip distribution over a long period and many machines, then pick a trade-off between detection delay and false positives. Better still, measure continuously and adapt: the Phi Accrual failure detector (used by Akka and Cassandra) tracks response times and their jitter and adjusts timeouts to the observed distribution, much as TCP's retransmission timeout does.

Synchronous Versus Asynchronous Networks

synchronous-versus-asynchronous-networks

Why not fix this in hardware — a network with a maximum delay that never drops packets? The fixed-line telephone network is exactly that, and extremely reliable. A call establishes a circuit: a fixed slice of bandwidth reserved along the whole route for the call's duration (ISDN: 4,000 frames/second, 16 bits per frame per direction — 16 bits every 250 µs, guaranteed). Because the space is pre-reserved at every hop, there is no queueing, so the maximum end-to-end latency is fixed: a bounded delay. Such a network is synchronous.

A TCP connection is nothing like a circuit. It opportunistically grabs whatever bandwidth is free, transfers a variable-sized blob as fast as it can, and uses nothing while idle. Ethernet and IP are packet-switched and have no concept of a circuit, so they queue and so their delays are unbounded. They are built that way because they're optimized for bursty traffic: a call needs a constant bit rate, but a web page, an email, or a file transfer has no natural bandwidth — you just want it done fast. Over a circuit you'd have to guess an allocation: too low wastes capacity and slows you; too high and the circuit can't be set up. Hybrids exist — ATM (nothing to do with cash machines), InfiniBand's link-level flow control — and with quality of service plus admission control you can emulate circuits or bound delay statistically, but that QoS is not enabled in multi-tenant datacenters, public clouds, or across the internet.

Unreliable Clocks

Applications ask clocks two different kinds of question. Durations: has this request timed out? what's the p99 response time? how many queries per second over the last five minutes? how long did the user stay? And points in time: when was this article published? when should the reminder go out? when does this cache entry expire? what's the timestamp on this log line? In a distributed system both are tricky. Messages take an unknown, variable time to travel, so "received" is always later than "sent" by an unknown amount — which makes it hard to say what happened in which order across machines. And every machine has its own hardware clock, a quartz oscillator that runs slightly fast or slow, so every machine has its own idea of the time. NTP (Network Time Protocol) adjusts a computer's clock toward a group of servers, which in turn get time from a more accurate source such as GPS.

FEYNMAN · two clocks, two jobs Your kitchen has a wall clock and an egg timer. The wall clock tells you what time it is; you'd set it from the radio, and if it was badly wrong you'd move the hands — including backwards. The egg timer tells you how long something took; its face has no dates, it only ever counts up, and comparing your egg timer's reading to your neighbor's is meaningless. Using the wall clock to time an egg is a bug the moment someone corrects it mid-boil. Using the egg timer to schedule a birthday is nonsense. Computers have both, and most clock bugs come from using the wrong one.

Monotonic Versus Time-of-Day Clocks

monotonic-versus-time-of-day-clocks

A time-of-day clock (wall-clock time) returns the current date and time per some calendar: clock_gettime(CLOCK_REALTIME) on Linux, System.currentTimeMillis() in Java — seconds or milliseconds since the Unix epoch, not counting leap seconds. It is synchronized with NTP, so a timestamp from one machine ideally means the same as one from another. But it has oddities: if the local clock is too far ahead of NTP it may be forcibly reset and jump backwards; it ignores leap seconds; and historically it was coarse (10 ms steps on old Windows). Those jumps make it unsuitable for measuring elapsed time.

A monotonic clock — clock_gettime(CLOCK_MONOTONIC), System.nanoTime() — is for durations: timeouts, response times. It is guaranteed to always move forward. Take a reading, do something, take another; the difference is the elapsed time. Its absolute value is meaningless (nanoseconds since boot, or something equally arbitrary), so comparing monotonic readings from two computers is nonsense. On multi-socket servers each CPU may have its own timer; the OS papers over the differences, but take monotonicity with a pinch of salt. NTP may slew the monotonic clock — speed it up or slow it down by up to 0.05% to correct quartz rate — but never make it jump. Resolution is usually microseconds or better. For timeouts in a distributed system a monotonic clock is the right tool: it assumes no synchronization between nodes and tolerates small inaccuracies.

Clock Synchronization and Accuracy

clock-synchronization-and-accuracy

Monotonic clocks need no synchronization; time-of-day clocks do, and the methods are less reliable than you'd hope. A catalogue of ways it goes wrong:

  • Drift. Quartz runs fast or slow, varying with temperature. Google assumes 200 ppm — 6 ms of drift between 30-second resyncs, or 17 seconds for a clock synced once a day. Drift caps the accuracy you can achieve even when everything works.
  • Forced resets. A clock too far from its NTP server may refuse to sync or be forcibly reset, so observers see time go backward or leap forward.
  • Silent firewalling. A node cut off from NTP by misconfiguration drifts unnoticed for a long time. This happens.
  • Network-limited accuracy. NTP can only be as good as the network delay: ~35 ms minimum error over the internet in one experiment, spikes to about a second under congestion; large delays can make the client give up.
  • Wrong servers. Some NTP servers report time off by hours. Clients query several and ignore outliers — but you are betting correctness on "a stranger on the internet."
  • Leap seconds. A minute with 59 or 61 seconds has crashed many large systems. The best fix may be to make NTP servers lie — smearing the extra second across a day — though server behaviour varies.
  • Virtual machines. The hardware clock is virtualized; when a core is shared, each VM is paused for tens of milliseconds, which the application sees as the clock jumping forward.
  • Devices you don't control. Phones and embedded devices may have clocks deliberately set wrong (to cheat game timers) — wildly past or future.

High accuracy is achievable if you invest: the draft MiFID II regulation requires high-frequency trading firms to keep clocks within 100 µs of UTC so flash crashes and manipulation can be reconstructed. That takes GPS receivers, the Precision Time Protocol (PTP), and careful deployment and monitoring — and it still fails if an NTP daemon is misconfigured or a firewall blocks the traffic, letting drift run away.

2ND EDITION · clocks got much better — for those who pay The 1st edition's clock section is NTP-centric with PTP as an exotic option. The 2nd edition reflects a decade of change: Precision Time Protocol at Meta (sub-microsecond datacenter time as a platform service), microsecond-accurate clocks on AWS EC2 (the ClockBound-style APIs that expose an error bound, i.e. TrueTime-like intervals for everyone), finance-driven synchronization requirements (MiFID II in force rather than draft), and new failure modes such as GPS jamming and spoofing knocking out the reference clocks everything else depends on. The lesson is sharpened rather than reversed: accurate time is now buyable, but only inside environments you control, and its error interval — not a point value — is what software should consume. Relying on Synchronized Clocks

relying-on-synchronized-clocks

Clocks look simple and hide pitfalls: a day may not have 86,400 seconds, time-of-day clocks move backward, and two nodes may disagree substantially. As with networks, software must assume clocks will occasionally be wrong and cope. The nasty part is that a bad clock goes unnoticed: a dead CPU or a broken network fails loudly and gets fixed; a drifting quartz or misconfigured NTP client leaves everything apparently working while the clock wanders off — and software relying on it produces silent, subtle data loss rather than a crash. So if you depend on synchronized clocks, monitor clock offsets across all machines and declare any node whose clock drifts too far dead, removing it before it does damage.

Timestamps for ordering events

The tempting, dangerous use: deciding which of two writes on different nodes came first. The book's Figure 8-3, in a multi-leader setup (Chapter 5←5): each write is stamped with the time-of-day clock of the node it originated on, and the receiving node keeps the write with the latest timestamp — last write wins (LWW), as used by Cassandra and Riak.

Could NTP be made accurate enough to prevent this? Probably not: NTP's accuracy is itself bounded by network round-trip time (plus drift), and to order events correctly the clock would need to be much more accurate than the thing being measured — network delay. Even with tight NTP sync, a packet can be sent at 100 ms by the sender's clock and arrive at 99 ms by the receiver's: it arrived before it was sent. The safer tool for ordering is a logical clock — an incrementing counter rather than a quartz crystal — which measures only relative order (before/after), not time of day. Time-of-day and monotonic clocks, which measure actual elapsed time, are physical clocks. Ordering returns in Chapter 9→9.

Clock readings have a confidence interval

A clock may report microseconds or nanoseconds, but that isn't its accuracy. Quartz drift is milliseconds even with a local NTP server every minute; over the public internet the best you get is tens of milliseconds, spiking past 100 ms under congestion. So a clock reading is not a point but a range: "95% confident the time is between 10.3 and 10.5 seconds past the minute." If you know the time only to ±100 ms, the microsecond digits are noise. The uncertainty is computable — from a GPS or atomic clock's manufacturer spec, or from expected drift since the last sync + the server's uncertainty + the round-trip time — but most systems don't expose it: clock_gettime() returns a number and no error bar, so you can't tell whether the interval is five milliseconds or five years. The exception is Google's TrueTime API in Spanner, which returns [earliest, latest]; the interval widens the longer since the local quartz was last synced to a better source.

Synchronized clocks for global snapshots

Snapshot isolation (Chapter 7←7) needs a monotonically increasing transaction ID: writes with a higher ID than the snapshot are invisible. On one node a counter suffices. Across many machines and datacenters, a global monotonic ID that also respects causality (if B read A's write, B's ID must exceed A's) requires coordination and becomes a bottleneck under many small transactions (Snowflake-style generators are only approximately monotonic and don't guarantee causal order). Could synchronized time-of-day timestamps serve as transaction IDs? Only if you handle the uncertainty — which is exactly what Spanner does.

Process Pauses

process-pauses

Another dangerous use of clocks. A single-leader partition must know it is still leader before accepting writes. One way is a lease from the other nodes — a lock with a timeout: only one holder at a time, valid until expiry, renewed periodically; if the holder dies it stops renewing and someone else takes over. The book's request loop renews when fewer than 10 seconds remain, then checks lease.isValid() and processes the request. Two things are wrong. First, the expiry was computed on another machine's clock and is compared to the local one — synchronized-clock dependence. Second, and even with a monotonic clock: the code assumes almost no time passes between the check and process(request). Normally true. But if the thread stops for 15 seconds around isValid(), the lease has expired and another node is leader by the time the request is processed — and nothing tells the thread it was paused, so it does something unsafe before the next loop iteration notices.

Is a 15-second pause plausible? Unfortunately yes, for many reasons:

CauseWhat happens
Garbage collectionStop-the-world pauses in the JVM and similar runtimes have lasted minutes; even "concurrent" collectors (HotSpot CMS) must stop the world sometimes. Tuning reduces but doesn't eliminate them.
VM suspend / live migrationA virtual machine can be suspended (memory saved to disk) and resumed at any point, for any length of time; live migration's pause depends on how fast the guest dirties memory.
Laptop lidEnd-user devices are suspended and resumed arbitrarily.
Context switch / steal timeThe OS or hypervisor can preempt the thread at any instruction; under heavy load it may wait a long time to run again (in a VM, time spent running other VMs is steal time).
Synchronous disk I/OA slow disk stalls the thread — even where no file access is visible in the code (the Java classloader loads classes lazily at first use). GC and I/O pauses can compound; a network block device (EBS) adds network variability.
Swapping / thrashingA page fault needs a disk read; under memory pressure the OS can spend most of its time paging. Servers often disable paging and prefer to kill a process.
SIGSTOPCtrl-Z or an operator's stray signal freezes the process until SIGCONT.

All of these preempt a thread at any point and resume it later without the thread noticing. It's like making single-machine code thread-safe — you can't assume anything about timing — except that the single-machine tools (mutexes, semaphores, atomics, blocking queues) don't carry over, because a distributed system has no shared memory, only messages over an unreliable network. A node must assume it can be paused for a long time at any point, even mid-function; meanwhile the world moves on and may declare it dead; and when it resumes it won't know it was asleep until it checks a clock.

FEYNMAN · the lease that outlived its holder's awareness You borrow a library book with a due date and promise yourself to renew it in time. Then you fall into a coma for two weeks. When you wake, you don't feel two weeks older; you pick up the book and keep reading, sincerely believing it's yours. Meanwhile the library reissued it to someone else. Every "I checked the lease and it was valid" in distributed code is a promise made before a possible coma. The fix in the next section is to make the library — not the borrower — enforce the rule.

Response time guarantees

Pauses can be eliminated if you try hard enough. Hard real-time systems — aircraft, rockets, cars, robots — have deadlines whose violation is a system failure (you don't want the airbag delayed by a GC pause). Achieving that takes support at every level: a real-time operating system with guaranteed CPU allocation, libraries documenting worst-case execution times, restricted or forbidden dynamic allocation (real-time GCs exist, but the app must keep them lightly loaded), and enormous testing. It sharply restricts languages and tools, is very expensive, and trades throughput for timeliness. For most server-side data systems it is neither economical nor appropriate, so they must live with pauses. ("Real-time" on the web — servers pushing data, stream processing (Chapter 11→11) — means something much vaguer.)

Limiting the impact of garbage collection

Short of real-time guarantees, GC pauses can be tamed. Treat a GC as a brief planned outage: the runtime warns the application that a collection is coming, the app stops sending that node new requests, lets it drain, collects with nothing in flight, then resumes — hiding pauses from clients and cutting tail latency (some financial trading systems do this). A variant: use the GC only for short-lived objects, which are cheap to collect, and restart processes periodically before enough long-lived garbage accumulates to need a full GC — one node at a time, traffic shifted away first, like a rolling upgrade. Neither eliminates pauses; both usefully reduce their impact.

knowledge-truth-and-lies

Knowledge, Truth, and Lies

No shared memory; only messages over a network with variable delays; partial failures, unreliable clocks, pauses. The consequences are disorienting: a node cannot know anything for sure. It can only guess from the messages it receives or fails to receive. It learns another node's state only by exchanging messages, and if the other node doesn't respond there is no way to know why, because network problems can't be distinguished from node problems. It borders on philosophy — what is true in our system, how sure can we be when perception itself is unreliable, should software obey cause and effect? — but we don't need to solve the meaning of life. We state our assumptions about behaviour (a system model) and design the system to meet them; algorithms can then be proved correct within that model. Reliable behaviour is achievable even when the model guarantees very little — it just isn't straightforward.

The Truth Is Defined by the Majority

the-truth-is-defined-by-the-majority

Three nightmares. A node with an asymmetric fault receives every message but all its outgoing ones are dropped; it works perfectly, yet nobody hears it, so after a timeout the others declare it dead — dragged to the graveyard screaming "I'm not dead!" to no one. A slightly better-informed node notices its messages aren't being acknowledged and realizes the network is at fault — and is still declared dead, and can do nothing about it. And a node in a one-minute stop-the-world GC: the others wait, retry, lose patience, declare it dead and load it on the hearse; then the GC ends and the node sits up in its coffin, chatting cheerfully, unaware a minute has passed. The moral: a node cannot trust its own judgment. A distributed system can't rely on any single node, because any node may fail at any time and leave the system stuck. So many algorithms rely on a quorum — voting among nodes (Chapter 5←5): decisions need a minimum number of votes, including the decision to declare a node dead. If a quorum says you're dead, you're dead, however alive you feel; you must step down. The usual quorum is an absolute majority: it tolerates failures (1 of 3, 2 of 5) yet stays safe, because there can only ever be one majority — two majorities can't make conflicting decisions at once. Quorums return in full with consensus in Chapter 9→9.

The leader and the lock

Systems often need exactly one of something: one leader per partition (no split brain), one holder of a lock, one owner of a username. A node may believe it is the chosen one and be wrong — it was the leader, then it was declared dead during a GC pause or network blip, demoted, and replaced. If it keeps acting as leader and others believe it, the system does something incorrect. The book's Figure 8-4 is a real HBase bug: clients take a lease from a lock service before writing a file in a storage service; client 1 pauses, its lease expires, client 2 gets a lease and writes; client 1 wakes still believing it holds the lease and writes too; the file is corrupted.

Fencing tokens

The fix is fencing (Figure 8-5). Every time the lock service grants a lease it also returns a fencing token: a number that increases with every grant. Every write to the protected resource must carry the writer's current token, and the resource itself rejects any write whose token is lower than one it has already seen. Client 1 has token 33 and pauses; client 2 gets 34 and writes; client 1 wakes and writes with 33 — the storage service has seen 34 and refuses. With ZooKeeper as the lock service, the zxid or node cversion serve as tokens, being monotonically increasing. The essential point: the check must happen on the server side — it's not enough for clients to check their own lock status, because a paused client's self-check is exactly what can't be trusted. For resources without native token support you can sometimes improvise (put the token in the filename). And server-side checking is arguably good hygiene anyway: services should protect themselves from clients, who are run by people with different priorities and are, in Caitie McCaffrey's phrase, jerks.

Byzantine Faults

byzantine-faults

Fencing stops a node that is inadvertently wrong. A node that wanted to subvert the system could just fake a token. This book assumes nodes are unreliable but honest: slow, silent, out of date — but if they respond, they follow the protocol to the best of their knowledge. If nodes may lie — send arbitrary corrupted responses, claim to have received a message they didn't — that's a Byzantine fault, and reaching agreement despite it is the Byzantine Generals Problem: n generals must agree on a plan by messenger, but some are traitors who send untrue messages while trying to stay undiscovered (the Two Generals Problem, with lossy messengers but no traitors, is the consensus problem of Chapter 9). Lamport chose "Byzantine" for its connotation of devious complexity, having been advised that "Albanian Generals" would offend.

A Byzantine fault-tolerant system keeps working when some nodes disobey the protocol or attackers interfere with the network. It matters in specific settings: aerospace, where radiation can corrupt memory and a failure means a crash, so flight control must tolerate arbitrary node behaviour; and systems with multiple mutually distrusting organizations, where a participant may try to cheat — Bitcoin and other blockchains are a way for untrusting parties to agree on whether a transaction happened without a central authority. In the datacenter systems of this book, you can usually assume no Byzantine faults: all nodes are yours, radiation is low, and BFT protocols are complicated and expensive (embedded ones lean on hardware support). Web apps do face malicious clients — hence input validation, sanitization, and output escaping against SQL injection and XSS — but the answer is to make the server the authority, not to run BFT. Two more limits: a software bug is technically a Byzantine fault, but if every node runs the same software, BFT (which needs a supermajority of more than two-thirds correct — at most 1 of 4 faulty) can't help unless you have four independent implementations; and BFT won't save you from a compromise either, since an attacker who owns one node probably owns them all. Authentication, access control, encryption, and firewalls remain the real defences.

Weak forms of lying

Still, pragmatic guards against weak lying — invalid messages from hardware faults, bugs, misconfiguration — are cheap and worthwhile even though they'd fold against a real adversary: application-level checksums, because corrupted packets sometimes slip past TCP/UDP checksums; input sanity checks even on internal services (range checks, string-size limits, protocol parsing); and multiple NTP servers, so a misconfigured one is spotted as an outlier and excluded.

2ND EDITION · Byzantine faults in the real world The 2nd edition updates this section with XFT (cross fault tolerance — a middle ground between crash-fault and full Byzantine tolerance that assumes the adversary can't control both nodes and the network at once) and with reports of real Byzantine incidents in production systems, moving the topic from "aerospace and blockchains" toward something operators of ordinary systems occasionally meet — corrupted-but-plausible messages from failing hardware, for instance. The book's stance survives: assume honest-but-unreliable nodes, add cheap sanity checks, and don't pay for BFT unless you truly have untrusting participants. System Model and Reality

system-model-and-reality

Algorithms for distributed problems (consensus, next chapter) must tolerate the faults of this chapter without depending on hardware and configuration details. That requires formalizing which faults are expected: a system model, an abstraction stating what an algorithm may assume. On timing, three models are common. Synchronous: bounded network delay, bounded pauses, bounded clock error — not exactly synchronized clocks, just known upper bounds; unrealistic, since unbounded delays do occur. Partially synchronous: behaves synchronously most of the time but occasionally blows past every bound, and when it does, delay, pauses, and clock error can be arbitrarily large — a realistic model of most systems. Asynchronous: no timing assumptions at all, not even a clock, so no timeouts; some algorithms work here, but it is very restrictive. On node failures, three more. Crash-stop: a node fails only by crashing and never returns. Crash-recovery: a node may crash and later resume; stable storage survives, in-memory state is lost. Byzantine: nodes may do anything. For real systems, partially synchronous with crash-recovery faults is generally the most useful model.

Correctness of an algorithm

Correctness is defined by properties, the way "sorted" means every left element is smaller than every right one. For a fencing-token generator: uniqueness (no two requests get the same token), monotonic sequence (if request x completed before y began, tx < ty), and availability (a requester that doesn't crash eventually gets a response). An algorithm is correct in a model if it satisfies its properties in every situation the model allows. But if every node crashes or every delay becomes infinite, nothing can get anything done — so how can that make sense?

Safety and liveness

By distinguishing two kinds of property. Uniqueness and monotonicity are safety properties; availability is a liveness property — the giveaway is the word "eventually" (yes, eventual consistency is a liveness property). Informally: safety is nothing bad happens, liveness is something good eventually happens; formally: if a safety property is violated you can point at the exact moment it broke, and the damage cannot be undone; a liveness property may not hold yet but can always still be satisfied in the future. The payoff: for distributed algorithms we demand that safety holds always, in every situation the model allows — even if all nodes crash or the whole network fails, the algorithm must never return a wrong result — while liveness may carry caveats: a response is required only if a majority of nodes are up and the network eventually recovers. The partially synchronous model itself promises that any interruption is finite and the system eventually returns to synchrony.

Mapping system models to the real world

Models are simplifications, and reality bites back. Crash-recovery assumes stable storage survives crashes — but disks get corrupted or wiped by hardware error or misconfiguration, and a firmware bug once made servers fail to see their drives on reboot. Quorum algorithms depend on a node remembering what it stored; a node with amnesia breaks the quorum condition and the algorithm's correctness. A model where stable storage "mostly" survives would be more faithful and harder to reason about. Theory may declare some things assumed impossible, but a real implementation still needs code for when they happen — even if it is printf("Sucks to be you"); exit(666) and a human cleans up. That, the book says, is arguably the line between computer science and software engineering. None of which makes models worthless: they distil real systems to a manageable set of faults, let us prove algorithms correct, and surface bugs that would hide for years in a real system until timing assumptions failed. Theoretical analysis and empirical testing are equally important.

FEYNMAN · what the chapter leaves you with Three facts and one move. Fact one: silence is ambiguous — no response means lost, dead, slow, or reply-lost, and you can't tell. Fact two: clocks are opinions — every machine has its own, corrected by jumps, and even a good one is a range, not a point. Fact three: any process may fall asleep mid-thought and wake up sure that nothing has changed. The move: never let one node's belief be authoritative. Take a majority vote; make the resource, not the client, enforce the rule (fencing); demand safety always and settle for liveness eventually. Chapter 9 is that move, done rigorously.

Core takeaways

  • Partial failure is the defining property of distributed systems: some parts broken unpredictably while others work, and nondeterministically — you may not even know whether an operation succeeded. A single computer hides physics behind an all-or-nothing model; a network exposes it.
  • Cloud vs supercomputer: HPC checkpoints and escalates any fault into a full stop-and-restart; internet services must stay online, run on commodity hardware where something is always broken, and so must build a reliable system from unreliable components — with a ceiling (TCP hides loss, not delay).
  • Networks are asynchronous: a request may be lost, queued, or hit a dead or paused node, and the response may be lost or delayed — all indistinguishable from "no response yet." The only detector is a timeout, which can't say whether the request was processed.
  • Faults are common (~12/month in one datacenter; redundancy doesn't fix human error; one-way link failures exist) and untested handling does arbitrary things — deadlocked clusters, deleted data. Induce faults deliberately.
  • Timeouts have no correct value: delay is unbounded because of queueing at switches, receiver OS, hypervisor, and TCP flow control plus retransmission; too short and you declare live nodes dead (double actions, cascading failure). Measure the RTT distribution and adapt (Phi Accrual, as in Cassandra/Akka).
  • Bounded delay is buyable, not free: circuit-switched telephone networks reserve bandwidth and never queue; packet switching wins for bursty traffic by sharing the wire dynamically. Latency vs utilization is one trade-off, the same one that time-shares CPUs; clouds choose cheap-and-variable.
  • Two clocks: time-of-day (NTP-synced, may jump backwards, ignores leap seconds — for points in time) and monotonic (only moves forward, slewed not stepped, absolute value meaningless — for durations and timeouts). Never measure a duration with the wall clock.
  • Clocks are inaccurate: 200 ppm drift (17 s/day unsynced), forced resets, firewalled NTP, tens of ms over the internet, wrong servers, leap seconds (smear them), virtualized clocks, untrusted devices. Bad clocks fail silently — monitor offsets and evict outliers. Accuracy to 100 µs (MiFID II) needs GPS/PTP and care.
  • Don't order events by timestamp: LWW with a 1 ms skew silently drops a causally later write; NTP can't be more accurate than the network delay it would need to resolve. Use logical clocks / version vectors for order. A clock reading is a confidence interval; Spanner's TrueTime exposes it and commit-waits its width so cross-datacenter snapshots stay causal.
  • Processes pause — GC (minutes), VM suspend/migration, steal time, disk and page-fault I/O, SIGSTOP — at any instruction, without noticing. A lease checked "just before" processing can have expired. Hard real-time systems eliminate pauses at great cost; servers instead treat GC as planned outages or restart before full GCs.
  • Truth is the majority: a node can't trust its own judgment (asymmetric partition, GC zombie); decisions, including "you're dead," need a quorum, usually an absolute majority — safe because only one majority can exist. Fencing tokens let the resource itself reject a stale lease holder; client-side checks cannot.
  • Byzantine faults (lying nodes) are assumed away in the datacenter — BFT is expensive, needs >⅔ honest, and can't help against a bug or compromise shared by all nodes; it matters for aerospace and mutually distrusting parties (blockchains). Cheap guards against weak lying: checksums, input sanity checks, multiple NTP servers.
  • System models: timing (synchronous / partially synchronous / asynchronous) × nodes (crash-stop / crash-recovery / Byzantine); partially synchronous + crash-recovery is the practical choice. Correctness = properties; safety (nothing bad, violated at an instant, irreversible) must hold always; liveness ("eventually") may hold conditionally. Models simplify reality (disks do lose data); theory and testing are both essential.
  • 2nd-edition arc (Ch 9, heavily revised): gray failure and asymmetric partitions as first-class faults; deterministic simulation testing and fault injection; modern clock infrastructure (PTP at Meta, µs-accurate EC2 clocks, GPS jamming) with error bounds exposed to software; XFT and real Byzantine incidents; fresh case studies (Roblox 2021, Cloudflare leap second, cable sabotage).

Q1 — c. HPC checkpoints, stops the whole cluster on a fault, and restarts from the checkpoint — partial failure escalated to total failure. Supercomputers have thousands of CPUs (a), their nodes do fail (b), and they use specialized networks (meshes, toruses), not commodity Ethernet (d).
Q2 — d. Figure 8-1. The sender's only information is the absence of a reply. (c) is the dangerous assumption: in the response-lost and node-paused cases the request was processed, so a retry does it twice.
Q3 — b. Gill et al.'s measurement study; redundancy addresses hardware failure, not the misconfiguration that causes many outages. (a) is a real effect in general but not the study's finding; (c) is a joke; (d) is false.
Q4 — a. Both consequences are in the text: duplicate actions (an email sent twice) and load transfer that can cascade until every node declares every other dead. (b)–(d) are fabricated.
Q5 — c. The formula needs both bounds; neither exists. The queueing sources are the switch outbound queue (Figure 8-2), the receiver's OS, the hypervisor, and TCP flow control and retransmission. (b) confuses TCP's internal RTO with application timeouts.
Q6 — b. The bandwidth-guess argument: too low is slow, too high can't be set up. (d) is backwards — circuits are more reliable. QoS and admission control can emulate circuits on packet networks but aren't enabled in multi-tenant clouds.
Q7 — d. Monotonic clocks (CLOCK_MONOTONIC, System.nanoTime()) exist for exactly this. (a) is the classic bug. (b) is true but irrelevant — a timeout is measured on one machine.
Q8 — a. 200 ppm × 86,400 s ≈ 17 s; the 6 ms figure (b) is for 30-second resyncs. NTP corrects periodically, not continuously (c).
Q9 — c. LWW compares wall-clock timestamps from different nodes; even excellent synchronization inverts the order of quick sequential writes, and a lagging-clock node can't overwrite a fast-clock node's values until the skew has elapsed. Fix: logical clocks / version vectors (Chapter 5).
Q10 — b. Non-overlapping intervals imply definite order; the commit wait manufactures non-overlap. (a) would reintroduce ambiguity; (c) is the coordination bottleneck Spanner avoids; (d) is false — this is how it provides cross-datacenter snapshots.
Q11 — d. The code assumes negligible time between check and action; a pause breaks that assumption and the thread has no way to notice. (c) is false by definition of monotonic; (b) is harmless.
Q12 — a. Figure 8-5: tokens increase per grant (ZooKeeper's zxid/cversion qualify), and the resource enforces ordering. Encryption (b) is unrelated — fencing assumes honest-but-mistaken clients, which is why deliberate token forgery is the Byzantine case.
Q13 — c. All four reasons are the book's. Byzantine faults do occur (a) — aerospace radiation, cheating participants — which is where BFT is justified (flight control, blockchains). Fencing (d) stops accidental error, not lies.
Q14 — b. Synchronous timing is unrealistic; crash-stop (nodes never return) is too pessimistic about recovery; Byzantine is unnecessarily hard for a single organization's datacenter.
Q15 — d. The "eventually" test marks availability as liveness. Safety violations are irreversible, so they're demanded unconditionally; liveness gets caveats. Eventual consistency, incidentally, is a liveness property too.
