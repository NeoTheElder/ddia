Replication

The major difference between a thing that might go wrong and a thing that cannot possibly go wrong is that when a thing that cannot possibly go wrong goes wrong it usually turns out to be impossible to get at or repair. — Douglas Adams, Mostly Harmless (1992)

Part I of the book lived on a single machine. Part II asks what happens when several machines share the job of storing and serving data — and this chapter is the first and gentlest of those questions: keep a copy of the same data on several nodes. If the data never changed, replication would be a one-time file copy. Every difficulty in this chapter comes from the same source: the data keeps changing while you copy it, and the copies have to agree about what happened and in what order. The chapter assumes the whole dataset fits on every node; Chapter 6→6 drops that assumption and splits the data up.

FEYNMAN · the whole chapter in one idea Imagine three clerks, each keeping their own copy of the same ledger, in three different buildings, communicating by messenger. Every design in this chapter is an answer to one question: who is allowed to write in the ledger, and how do the other copies find out? One clerk writes and the others copy (single-leader). Several clerks write and reconcile later (multi-leader). Anyone writes anywhere and readers ask several clerks and compare (leaderless). Each answer trades away something different — write latency, durability, simplicity, or the very notion of "the latest value." And the messengers are slow and sometimes lost, so a reader who asks the wrong clerk gets yesterday's ledger: that is replication lag, and half the chapter is about what it does to users. Colors as always: ember = writes and the replication stream, cyan = reads.
distributed-data (Part II framing)

Part II: why distribute at all?

Kleppmann opens Part II with a Feynman epigraph of his own — "reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled" — and a plain list of reasons you'd spread a database across machines. Scalability: data volume, read load, or write load outgrows one box. Fault tolerance / high availability: the application must keep working when a machine, a network, or a whole datacenter dies. Latency: users around the world should be served from nearby, not from across an ocean.

Scaling to higher load: three architectures

The simplest path is a bigger machine — vertical scaling or scaling up. Many CPUs, RAM chips, and disks under one operating system with a fast interconnect form a shared-memory architecture: the components behave as one machine. The problem is that cost grows faster than linearly (twice the CPUs costs well over twice the money and, because of bottlenecks, gives less than twice the throughput), fault tolerance is limited (hot-swappable parts, but one building), and even inside a big machine, NUMA means memory near one CPU is far from another — so partitioning creeps back in anyway. The shared-disk architecture gives each machine its own CPUs and RAM but a shared disk array over a fast network (NAS/SAN); it survives in some data warehousing, but locking contention caps its scalability.

Shared-nothing architectures

The alternative that took over is shared-nothing (horizontal scaling, scaling out): each machine or VM running the database is a node with its own CPUs, RAM, and disks, and all coordination happens in software over an ordinary network. No special hardware, so you buy whatever has the best price/performance; data can spread across geographic regions to cut latency and to survive losing a datacenter; and with cloud VMs, even a small company can afford a multi-region deployment. The book focuses on shared-nothing not because it's always the right choice but because it demands the most care: distributing data across nodes brings constraints and trade-offs that the database cannot magically hide, it adds application complexity, and it sometimes limits the data models you can use — the "COST" paper's single-threaded program beating a 100-core cluster is the cautionary tale. This is the same scale-up-vs-scale-out pragmatism that Chapter 1←1 introduced.

Replication versus partitioning

Two mechanisms distribute data across nodes, and they are separate ideas that usually travel together. Replication: keep a copy of the same data on several nodes, possibly in different places — redundancy when nodes fail, and often better performance (this chapter). Partitioning (also sharding): split one big database into subsets called partitions and put different partitions on different nodes (Chapter 6→6). Figure II-1 in the book shows the combination: two partitions, two replicas each. After these two chapters Part II moves to transactions→7, then to the fundamental limits of distributed systems in Chapter 8→8 and Chapter 9→9.

leaders-and-followers

Leaders and Followers

Every node holding a copy of the database is a replica. With several replicas the unavoidable question is: how does every write reach every replica? The most common answer is leader-based replication — also called active/passive or master–slave replication:

  1. One replica is the leader (master, primary). Clients send all writes to it; it writes to its local storage first.
  2. The other replicas are followers (read replicas, slaves, secondaries, hot standbys). Whenever the leader writes, it also sends the change to every follower as part of a replication log or change stream. Each follower applies the writes in the same order the leader processed them.
  3. Clients may read from the leader or any follower — but writes are accepted only at the leader; followers are read-only from the client's point of view.

It's built into PostgreSQL (since 9.0), MySQL, Oracle Data Guard, and SQL Server's AlwaysOn Availability Groups; into MongoDB, RethinkDB, and Espresso; and beyond databases into Kafka and RabbitMQ's highly available queues — the same message brokers Chapter 4←4 used for dataflow. Replicated block devices like DRBD are similar. (Terminology footnote: in PostgreSQL a hot standby serves reads, a warm standby only applies changes; the book doesn't need the distinction.) Replication is an old topic — the principles were worked out in the 1970s and the constraints of networks haven't changed — but mainstream use of distributed databases is recent, which is why eventual consistency is so widely misunderstood.

FEYNMAN · one pen, many photocopiers A single-leader system is an office with exactly one pen. Anyone who wants to change the ledger hands their change to the person holding the pen; that person writes it down and then feeds the page through the photocopiers, one for each other office. Anyone can read any office's copy. The one-pen rule buys you the most valuable thing in this chapter — a single, total order of writes, so no two offices ever disagree about what happened first. Everything you'll pay for later (failover, lag) is the price of that one pen.

Synchronous Versus Asynchronous Replication

synchronous-versus-asynchronous-replication

When the leader forwards a change to a follower, does it wait for the follower's confirmation before telling the client the write succeeded? In relational databases this is usually a configuration option; elsewhere it's often hardcoded. The book's Figure 5-2 traces a user updating a profile picture with one synchronous and one asynchronous follower — here it is as a stepper:

So in practice "synchronous replication" means one follower is synchronous and the rest asynchronous; if the sync follower gets slow or unavailable, an async follower is promoted to sync. This guarantees an up-to-date copy on at least two nodes (leader + one follower) and is called semi-synchronous. Just as often, leader-based replication is configured fully asynchronous: if the leader fails unrecoverably, writes not yet replicated are lost — a write can be confirmed to the client and still not be durable. In exchange the leader keeps accepting writes even if every follower falls behind. Weakened durability sounds like a bad deal, yet fully async is widespread, especially with many or geographically scattered followers.

Research on replication

Because async data loss is a real problem, researchers keep looking for schemes that lose nothing but still perform. Chain replication is a synchronous variant used in Microsoft Azure Storage. And there is a deep connection between replica consistency and consensus — getting several nodes to agree on a value — which Chapter 9→9 explores; this chapter stays with the simpler forms databases actually use.

Handling Node Outages

handling-node-outages

Nodes go down — unexpectedly through faults, and just as often on purpose (rebooting for a kernel security patch). Being able to restart individual nodes without downtime is a huge operational win, so the goal is: keep the system as a whole running, and keep the blast radius of any single outage small. With leader-based replication the two cases are very different.

Follower failure: catch-up recovery

Easy. Each follower keeps its received log on local disk. After a crash or a network blip, it knows the last transaction it applied, reconnects to the leader, requests everything since, applies it, and is caught up — exactly the tail end of the new-follower procedure above.

Leader failure: failover

Hard. A follower must be promoted to leader, clients must be reconfigured to send writes to it, and the other followers must start consuming from it. This is failover, done manually (an administrator is paged and does the steps) or automatically:

Implementation of Replication Logs

implementation-of-replication-logs

What exactly travels from leader to follower? Four answers are used in practice, and the differences matter for correctness, for upgrades, and for who else can read the stream.

Statement-based replication

The leader logs every write statement it executes — each INSERT, UPDATE, DELETE — and followers parse and run the same SQL as if a client had sent it. Compact, but it breaks in several ways: nondeterministic functions (NOW(), RAND()) produce different values on each replica; statements that depend on existing data or autoincrement columns must run in exactly the same order, which is hard with concurrent transactions; and side effects (triggers, stored procedures, UDFs) may differ per replica unless perfectly deterministic. Workarounds exist — the leader can substitute fixed values for nondeterministic calls when logging — but the edge cases are endless. MySQL used it before 5.1 and now switches to row-based replication when it detects nondeterminism; VoltDB keeps statement-based replication safe by requiring deterministic transactions.

Write-ahead log (WAL) shipping

Chapter 3←3 showed that every storage engine already appends every write to a log: the segment files of an LSM engine, or the write-ahead log that a B-tree←3 writes before touching a page. Either way it's an append-only byte sequence describing all writes — so the leader can simply send that same log over the network, and the follower rebuilds byte-identical data structures. PostgreSQL and Oracle do this. The drawback: the WAL is very low level (which bytes changed in which disk block), so replication is welded to the storage engine and its version. If the on-disk format changes between releases, leader and follower can't run different versions — and that kills the zero-downtime upgrade trick (upgrade followers first, fail over to one, upgrade the old leader). With WAL shipping, upgrades often mean downtime.

Logical (row-based) log replication

Use a different format for replication than for storage — a logical log decoupled from engine internals. For a relational database it's a sequence of row-granularity records: an insert carries all column values; a delete carries enough to identify the row (primary key, or all old values if there's none); an update carries the identifier plus new values (at least of changed columns). A multi-row transaction produces several records followed by a commit record. MySQL's binlog in row mode works this way. Because it's decoupled, it stays backward compatible across versions and even across storage engines — and it's easy for external programs to parse: feeding a data warehouse, building custom indexes and caches. That is change data capture, which Chapter 11→11 builds a whole architecture on.

Trigger-based replication

Everything above is done by the database, invisible to application code. Sometimes you need more flexibility — replicate only a subset, replicate into a different kind of database, or apply custom conflict resolution — and replication moves up into the application. Tools like Oracle GoldenGate read the database log for you; otherwise use triggers and stored procedures: a trigger fires on a write and logs the change into a separate table, an external process reads that table and does whatever replication logic it likes. Databus for Oracle and Bucardo for Postgres work this way. Higher overhead and more bug-prone than built-in replication — but flexible.

problems-with-replication-lag

Problems with Replication Lag

Fault tolerance is only one reason to replicate; the Part II intro also promised scalability and latency. Leader-based replication funnels every write through one node, but reads can go anywhere — and web workloads are mostly reads. So the tempting read-scaling architecture: many followers, reads spread across them, load lifted off the leader, users served from a nearby replica. Add capacity by adding followers. This only works with asynchronous replication: synchronous to all followers would mean any one node or network failure blocks every write, and with more nodes, something is always down.

The price: a read from a lagging follower returns stale data. Run the same query on leader and follower at the same instant and you may get different answers. It's a temporary state — stop writing and the followers catch up — hence eventual consistency (a term coined by Terry et al., popularized by Werner Vogels, adopted as the battle cry of NoSQL; but note that async followers in a relational database are just as eventually consistent). "Eventually" is deliberately vague: there is no bound on lag. In normal operation it's a fraction of a second; near capacity or during network trouble it's seconds or minutes, and then the anomalies stop being theoretical. Reading Your Own Writes

reading-your-own-writes

The pattern behind Act 1: users submit data (a record, a comment) and then view it — writes must go to the leader, but the view is a read that could come from a follower, which is exactly right for data read often and written rarely. With asynchronous replication the user may look before the follower has caught up and conclude their data was lost. Read-after-write consistency (read-your-writes) promises that a user who reloads always sees updates they themselves submitted — nothing about other users' updates. The techniques, from the stepper: read anything the user may have modified from the leader (needs a rule that doesn't require querying — "your own profile is only editable by you, so read it from the leader; everyone else's from a follower"); if most data is user-editable that defeats read scaling, so instead use time — reads go to the leader for a minute after the user's last update, and followers more than a minute behind are excluded; or the client remembers the timestamp of its last write (logical, like a log sequence number, or wall-clock — in which case clock synchronization becomes critical, see Chapter 8→8) and the serving replica must reflect updates at least to that point, else another replica serves the read or it waits. Multi-datacenter deployments add routing complexity, since leader-bound requests must reach the leader's datacenter. Cross-device read-after-write (type on your laptop, see it on your phone) is harder still: the last-update timestamp lives on one device and must be centralized, and different devices may take entirely different network routes to different datacenters.

Monotonic Reads

monotonic-reads

Act 2: reads from different replicas can make a user see time move backward — a refresh routed to a more-lagged follower makes a comment disappear. Monotonic reads guarantees this doesn't happen: weaker than strong consistency, stronger than eventual consistency. You may see stale data, but a user making several reads in sequence never reads older data after newer. Implementation: make each user read from the same replica, chosen by hash of user ID rather than randomly (different users can use different replicas); if that replica fails, reroute.

Consistent Prefix Reads

consistent-prefix-reads

Act 3 is about causality: Mrs Cake's answer depends on Mr Poons's question, and an observer must never see the answer first. Consistent prefix reads guarantees that if writes happen in some order, readers see them in that order. It's specifically a partitioned-database problem (Chapter 6→6): with one leader applying every write in one order, reads trivially see a consistent prefix; but independent partitions have no global ordering, so a reader may see some partitions in an older state and others newer. Fixes: write causally related data to the same partition (not always efficient), or track causal dependencies explicitly — the "happens-before" machinery in Detecting Concurrent Writes and the ordering guarantees→9 of Chapter 9.

FEYNMAN · three promises about a bulletin board Think of followers as bulletin boards in different hallways, updated by a runner. Eventual consistency says only "the boards will match — at some point." The three guarantees are three specific, cheap promises you can make on top. Read-your-writes: after you pin up a notice, you'll never walk to a board and find yours missing (we'll send you to a board the runner has already visited). Monotonic reads: you'll never see a notice, walk to another board, and find it not there yet (we'll always send you to the same board). Consistent prefix: you'll never see the reply pinned up before the notice it replies to (related notices travel together). None of these is "everyone sees everything instantly" — they're the minimum a user needs to not feel the system is lying to them.

Solutions for Replication Lag

solutions-for-replication-lag

Designing on an eventually consistent system, ask: what happens to the application if lag grows to minutes or hours? If "nothing bad" — great. If users get a bad experience, design for a stronger guarantee such as read-after-write. Pretending replication is synchronous when it isn't is a recipe for trouble. The application can provide stronger guarantees than its database (leader reads for certain queries), but handling this in application code is complex and easy to get wrong. It would be better if the database just "did the right thing" — and that is what transactions are for: a way for the database to offer stronger guarantees so the application can be simpler. Single-node transactions have existed for decades; many distributed databases abandoned them, claiming they're too costly for performance and availability and that eventual consistency is inevitable at scale. There's some truth there, but it's oversimplified — the book builds a more nuanced view in Chapter 7→7 and Chapter 9, and looks at alternatives in Part III.

multi-leader-replication

Multi-Leader Replication

Single-leader replication has one big downside: one leader, and all writes go through it. If you can't reach it — a network interruption between you and it — you can't write. (In a partitioned database each partition has a leader, possibly on different nodes, but still exactly one per partition.) The natural extension: let more than one node accept writes. Replication proceeds as before — each node that processes a write forwards it to all the others — and this is a multi-leader configuration (master–master, active/active). Each leader is simultaneously a follower of the other leaders.

Use Cases for Multi-Leader Replication

use-cases-for-multi-leader-replication

Within a single datacenter it rarely makes sense — the benefits seldom outweigh the complexity. Three situations justify it.

Multi-datacenter operation

With replicas in several datacenters (to survive a datacenter failure, or to be close to users), single-leader means the leader lives in one datacenter and every write crosses the internet to it. Multi-leader puts a leader in each datacenter: within a datacenter, ordinary leader–follower replication; between datacenters, each leader replicates to the other leaders.

Some databases support this natively; often it's bolted on by external tools — Tungsten Replicator for MySQL, BDR for PostgreSQL, GoldenGate for Oracle. The big downside is written on the figure: the same data can be modified concurrently in two datacenters, and those write conflicts must be resolved. And since multi-leader is often a retrofitted feature, it has subtle configuration pitfalls and surprising interactions with autoincrementing keys, triggers, and integrity constraints — enough that it's widely regarded as dangerous territory to avoid if you can.

Clients with offline operation

The calendar on your phone, laptop, and tablet: you must be able to see meetings (reads) and add them (writes) with or without connectivity, and offline changes must sync when you're back online. Every device holds a local database that acts as a leader, with asynchronous multi-leader replication (the "sync") between all replicas of your calendar. Replication lag may be hours or days. Architecturally this is multi-datacenter replication taken to the extreme — each device is a datacenter and the network between them is dreadful — and the rich history of broken calendar sync shows how hard it is. CouchDB was designed for exactly this mode.

Collaborative editing

Etherpad and Google Docs let several people edit one document at once. It doesn't look like database replication, but it shares everything with the offline case: a user's edit is applied instantly to their local replica (the document state in their browser) and asynchronously replicated to the server and other editors. Guaranteeing no conflicts means locking the document before editing — which is just single-leader replication with transactions on the leader. For fast collaboration you make the unit of change tiny (a keystroke) and skip locks, which allows simultaneous editing and brings every multi-leader challenge, conflict resolution included.

Handling Write Conflicts

handling-write-conflicts

The biggest problem with multi-leader replication. The book's Figure 5-7: two users edit a wiki page's title concurrently — user 1 changes A→B at leader 1, user 2 changes A→C at leader 2. Each write succeeds locally; the conflict surfaces only when the changes are replicated. It cannot happen in a single-leader database.

Conflict avoidance

The simplest strategy — and, since many implementations resolve conflicts badly, the most recommended: make sure all writes to a given record go through the same leader. Route each user to a "home" datacenter (by geography, say) and do all their reads and writes through its leader; from any one user's viewpoint the system is single-leader. It breaks down when you must change a record's home — a datacenter fails, or the user moves — and then concurrent writes on different leaders are back on the table.

Automatic conflict resolution

Hand-written rules get complicated and buggy fast. Amazon's shopping cart is the canonical cautionary tale: for a while its handler preserved items added to a cart but not items removed, so customers saw deleted items reappear. Research toward doing this automatically: conflict-free replicated datatypes (CRDTs) — sets, maps, ordered lists, counters that many users can edit concurrently and that resolve conflicts sensibly on their own (Riak 2.0 ships some); mergeable persistent data structures — Git-like explicit history with three-way merges (CRDTs use two-way); and operational transformation — the algorithm behind Etherpad and Google Docs, designed for concurrent editing of an ordered list such as the characters of a document. Young in databases in 2017, the book predicted wider adoption — see the 2nd-edition aside above.

What is a conflict?

Two writes to the same field, different values — obviously a conflict. Others are subtler. A meeting-room booking system must never let one room be booked by two groups at the same time; two bookings for the same room and slot created on two different leaders conflict even though each passed an availability check — the check ran against a replica that hadn't seen the other booking. No ready-made answer; Chapter 7→7 has more conflict examples (this one is write skew in disguise) and Chapter 12→12 discusses scalable detection and resolution.

FEYNMAN · why "last write wins" is a lie about time Two people in different cities cross out a word in their copies of a shared document at the "same time." Which edit is later? You could compare their wristwatches — but wristwatches disagree, and anyway neither person knew about the other's edit, so there was no "later"; the edits were concurrent. LWW answers a question that has no answer by inventing one from clock readings, and the losing edit silently ceases to exist even though its author was told "saved." That's fine for a cache. It's not fine for a shopping cart, a booking, or a medical record. The rest of the chapter builds the honest alternative: detect concurrency and keep both.

Multi-Leader Replication Topologies

multi-leader-replication-topologies

A replication topology is the set of communication paths along which writes propagate. Two leaders have one plausible topology; with more, choices appear.

In circular and star topologies a write passes through several nodes, so nodes forward changes they receive from others. To stop infinite loops every node gets a unique ID and each write in the log is tagged with the IDs of every node it has traversed; a node that receives a change carrying its own ID drops it. The weakness: one failed node interrupts the flow between others until fixed, and rerouting is usually manual. All-to-all's dense connectivity avoids the single point of failure — but has its own trouble: some links are faster than others, so messages can overtake each other.

Conflict detection in many multi-leader products is poor: at the time of writing PostgreSQL BDR did not provide causal ordering of writes, and Tungsten Replicator for MySQL didn't even try to detect conflicts. If you run multi-leader, read the documentation carefully and test that the guarantees you believe you have are the ones you actually get.

leaderless-replication

Leaderless Replication

Single- and multi-leader replication share an idea: a client sends a write to one node (a leader), the system copies it onward, and a leader decides the order in which writes apply. Some systems abandon the leader entirely and let any replica accept writes directly. The earliest replicated systems were leaderless; the idea was forgotten during the relational era and revived when Amazon built its in-house Dynamo — hence Dynamo-style for Riak, Cassandra, and Voldemort, the open-source datastores it inspired. (Confusingly, AWS's DynamoDB is a different, single-leader architecture.) In some implementations the client sends writes to several replicas itself; in others a coordinator node does it on the client's behalf — but unlike a leader, that coordinator imposes no ordering on writes. That one difference changes everything about how the database is used.

Writing to the Database When a Node Is Down

Three replicas, one being rebooted. Leader-based: if the leader is the one down, you need a failover to keep writing. Leaderless: there is no failover. The book's Figure 5-10 as a stepper:

Quorums for reading and writing

n, w, r are normally configurable in Dynamo-style databases. A common choice: n odd (3 or 5), w = r = (n+1)/2 rounded up. But you can tune: a read-heavy, write-light workload might set w = n, r = 1 — fast reads, but one failed node stops all writes. There may be more than n nodes in the cluster — any given value lives on only n of them, which is what lets the dataset be partitioned across more machines than one value's replicas (Chapter 6→6). Availability arithmetic: w < n tolerates an unavailable node for writes; r < n for reads; n=3, w=2, r=2 tolerates one node down; n=5, w=3, r=3 tolerates two. Below w or r available nodes, the operation returns an error. "Unavailable" covers everything — crashed, disk full, network partition between client and node — we only care whether a success response came back.

FEYNMAN · the pigeonhole guarantee Five mailboxes, and you post a letter into any three (w = 3). Later you open any three (r = 3). Since 3 + 3 > 5, at least one mailbox you open must be one you posted to — there aren't enough boxes for the two sets to miss each other. That is the entire quorum argument: no leader, no ordering, just counting. Every subtlety in the next section comes from the assumptions hidden in "you posted to three" — that they were the designated five boxes, that nobody else was posting a different letter at the same moment, and that a box didn't burn down and get rebuilt empty.

Limitations of Quorum Consistency

limitations-of-quorum-consistency

With w + r > n you generally read the latest value, because the write set and read set overlap. Usually r and w are majorities (more than n/2), which satisfies the condition while tolerating up to n/2 failures — but quorums needn't be majorities; only the overlap matters, which leaves room for other assignments (Flexible Paxos exploits exactly this). You can also set w + r ≤ n on purpose: reads and writes still go to all n nodes but wait for fewer replies — more stale reads, but lower latency and higher availability, since the database only becomes unavailable when reachable replicas drop below w or r.

Even with w + r > n, edge cases return stale values:

  • Sloppy quorums (below): the w writes may land on different nodes than the r reads, so overlap is no longer guaranteed.
  • Concurrent writes: it's unclear which happened first; the only safe move is to merge them. Picking a winner by timestamp (LWW) loses writes to clock skew.
  • A write concurrent with a read: the write may be on only some replicas; whether the read sees old or new is undetermined.
  • Partial write failure: a write that succeeded on some replicas but fewer than w overall is not rolled back where it succeeded — so a write reported as failed may or may not show up in later reads.
  • Restoring from a stale replica: a node with the new value fails and is rebuilt from a node with the old value; now fewer than w nodes hold the new value and the quorum condition is broken.
  • Unlucky timing even when everything works — the linearizability-and-quorums case in Chapter 9→9.

So quorums appear to guarantee reading the latest value, but in practice it isn't so simple. Dynamo-style databases are optimized for use cases that tolerate eventual consistency; w and r tune the probability of staleness, not an absolute guarantee. In particular you usually do not get read-your-writes, monotonic reads, or consistent prefix reads — those anomalies can all occur. Stronger guarantees need transactions or consensus (Chapters 7 and 9).

Monitoring staleness

Operationally you must know whether your database is returning current results, even if the application tolerates staleness — a replica far behind means something is wrong (network, overloaded node). Leader-based systems make this easy: writes apply in the same order on leader and followers, each node has a position in the log, and leader position − follower position is the lag. Leaderless systems have no fixed order, so it's harder — and with read repair only (no anti-entropy) there is no bound on how old a rarely read value can be. Research (Bailis et al.'s PBS) predicts the expected percentage of stale reads from n, w, r; it isn't common practice, but staleness belongs in every database's standard metrics. Eventual consistency is deliberately vague — for operability you want to quantify "eventual."

Sloppy Quorums and Hinted Handoff

sloppy-quorums-and-hinted-handoff

Well-configured quorums tolerate node failures without failover and tolerate slow nodes because you only wait for w or r replies — attractive for high availability, low latency, and occasional staleness. But a network interruption can cut a client off from many nodes at once. Those nodes are alive and reachable by other clients, but to this client they may as well be dead, and it may no longer reach w or r of the n "home" nodes for a value. In a large cluster (many more than n nodes) the client can probably reach some nodes — just not the right ones. The designers' choice: return errors for everything that can't reach a quorum, or accept writes anyway onto reachable nodes that aren't among the value's designated n?

Sloppy quorums are great for write availability: as long as any w nodes are up, writes succeed. But they break the reading guarantee: even with w + r > n, the latest value may sit on nodes outside n until handoff completes, so a read of r home nodes can miss it. A sloppy quorum isn't a quorum in the traditional sense at all — only a durability assurance that the data is on w nodes somewhere. Optional everywhere: on by default in Riak, off by default in Cassandra and Voldemort.

Multi-datacenter operation

Leaderless replication suits multi-datacenter use too, since it's built to tolerate conflicting concurrent writes, network interruptions, and latency spikes. Cassandra and Voldemort keep it inside the normal model: n counts replicas across all datacenters and you configure how many live in each; each write goes to all replicas everywhere, but the client waits only for a quorum within its local datacenter, so cross-datacenter delays don't hurt it, and the remote writes are typically asynchronous. Riak instead keeps all client–node traffic within one datacenter (n is per datacenter) and replicates between clusters asynchronously in the background — much like multi-leader replication.

Detecting Concurrent Writes

detecting-concurrent-writes

Dynamo-style databases let several clients write the same key concurrently, so conflicts occur even with strict quorums — like multi-leader replication, plus extra conflict sources during read repair and hinted handoff. The core problem: events arrive in different orders at different nodes because of variable delays and partial failures. The book's Figure 5-12: clients A and B both write key X across three nodes. Node 1 gets A's write and never B's (transient outage). Node 2 gets A then B. Node 3 gets B then A. If each node just overwrites on arrival, node 2 ends with B and the others with A — permanently inconsistent. Replicas must converge, and most implementations leave the how to you, the application developer, so you need to understand the internals.

Last write wins (discarding concurrent writes)

One route to convergence: each replica keeps only the most "recent" value and discards "older" ones — given some unambiguous way to say which is more recent. The quotes are the point: in Figure 5-12 neither client knew about the other, so neither write happened "first"; they are concurrent and their order is undefined. You can force an order — attach a timestamp, biggest wins, discard the rest — and that is last write wins (LWW), Cassandra's only conflict resolution method and an option in Riak. It converges, at the cost of durability: several concurrent writes to one key, each acknowledged as successful (written to w replicas), and only one survives; the rest are silently gone. Worse, LWW can drop writes that aren't concurrent, because of clock skew (Chapter 8→8). Acceptable for caching; a poor choice when losing data isn't. The only safe way to use LWW is to make every key immutable — write once, never update — e.g. Cassandra's recommendation of a UUID per write as the key.

The "happens-before" relationship and concurrency

How do we decide whether two operations are concurrent? In Figure 5-9 earlier, B's update happened after A's insert — B's operation builds on A's (the row B updated is the row A inserted); B is causally dependent on A. In Figure 5-12, neither client knew of the other when it started, so there's no causal dependency: the writes are concurrent. Definition: A happens before B if B knows about A, depends on A, or builds on A in some way. Two operations are concurrent if neither happens before the other — neither knows about the other. For any two operations A and B there are exactly three possibilities: A before B, B before A, or concurrent. If one happened before the other, the later should overwrite the earlier; if concurrent, there's a conflict to resolve. What we need is an algorithm to tell which case we're in.

FEYNMAN · concurrency, time, and relativity "Concurrent" sounds like "at the same instant," but exact time is irrelevant — and, with the unreliable clocks of Chapter 8, unknowable. Two operations are concurrent if they were unaware of each other, whatever the wall clock says. The book draws the analogy to special relativity: information can't travel faster than light, so two events far enough apart in space and close enough in time cannot have influenced each other. In computers the "speed of light" is far slower and erratic — a slow or interrupted network means two writes minutes apart can still be concurrent, because neither could have known about the other. Causality, not the clock, is what defines "before."

Capturing the happens-before relationship

Start with a single replica and work up. The book's Figure 5-13: two clients add items to the same shopping cart (or two air-traffic controllers add aircraft to a sector, if groceries feel too inane). The server keeps a version number per key. Walk it:

Merging concurrently written values

No data is silently dropped — but clients do the cleanup, merging concurrent values (Riak calls them siblings). This is the same problem as multi-leader conflict resolution. Picking one by version or timestamp (LWW) loses data; the smarter application-level approach for a cart is the union: [milk, flour, eggs, bacon] ∪ [eggs, milk, ham] → milk, flour, eggs, bacon, ham without duplicates. But if users can also remove items, union is wrong: an item removed in only one sibling reappears in the merge — Amazon's cart bug. The fix is that a removal can't just delete; it leaves a marker with a version number, a tombstone (the same idea as in Chapter 3's log compaction←3), so the merge knows the item was deleted. Because merging in application code is complex and error-prone, data structures that merge themselves — Riak's CRDT datatypes — do it automatically, deletions included.

Version vectors

The cart used one replica. With multiple leaderless replicas a single version number per key isn't enough: we need a version number per replica and per key. Each replica increments its own counter on a write and also tracks the counters it has seen from every other replica; the collection is a version vector, and it tells the database which values to overwrite and which to keep as siblings. The most interesting variant is the dotted version vector used in Riak 2.0. Like the single version number, version vectors travel from database to client on reads and back on writes (Riak calls the encoded string a causal context), and they make it safe to read from one replica and write to another — siblings may result, but nothing is lost if they're merged correctly.

2ND EDITION · version vectors vs vector clocks, and where LWW sits The 1st edition's sidebar warns that version vectors are "sometimes also called vector clocks" but aren't quite the same, and defers to the references. The 2nd edition draws the line more explicitly — version vectors compare the state of replicas (the right tool here); vector clocks order events — and gives dotted version vectors a fuller treatment alongside the CRDT material. Flexible Paxos appears in the quorum discussion as the formal statement that read and write quorums need only intersect, not be majorities. [inferred from the 2nd-edition references repo]
FEYNMAN · the three replication families, side by side Single-leader: one pen. Easy to understand, no conflicts, a total order of writes for free — but writes have one address, and if the pen-holder vanishes you need a (fraught) succession. Multi-leader: a pen per office. Writes are local and fast, offices work through network outages, but two offices will write different things in the same cell and you must decide, convergently, what the cell says. Leaderless: no pen, just counting. Post to w boxes, open r boxes, w + r > n. No failover, great availability, but "latest" stops being a fact and becomes something you compute from versions — and siblings are yours to merge. The book's verdict: single-leader is popular because it's simple; the other two are more robust to faulty nodes, interruptions, and latency spikes, at the price of being harder to reason about and offering only weak consistency.

Core takeaways

  • Why replicate: high availability (survive node/datacenter loss), disconnected operation, latency (data near users), and read scalability. Part II frames it as shared-nothing nodes; replication (same data, many nodes) and partitioning (different data, different nodes) are orthogonal and combined.
  • Single-leader: all writes to one leader, which streams a replication log to followers; reads anywhere. Built into PostgreSQL, MySQL, Oracle, SQL Server, MongoDB, Kafka, RabbitMQ. Its gift is a total order of writes.
  • Sync vs async: a synchronous follower guarantees a second up-to-date copy but one dead sync follower blocks all writes — so practice is semi-synchronous (one sync follower) or fully async, which risks losing acknowledged writes on leader failure.
  • Operations: new followers come from a snapshot pinned to a log position plus catch-up; follower recovery is catch-up from the local log; failover = detect (timeout) → choose (election, most up-to-date) → reconfigure — and it's fraught: discarded unreplicated writes (GitHub's key reuse), split brain, the timeout dilemma. Many teams fail over manually.
  • Replication logs: statement-based (nondeterminism), WAL shipping (exact but engine/version-coupled ⇒ upgrade downtime), logical/row-based (decoupled, cross-version, parseable ⇒ change data capture), trigger-based (flexible, costly).
  • Replication lag ⇒ eventual consistency with no bound on "eventually." Three anomalies and their guarantees: read-after-write (see your own writes: leader reads, time windows, last-write timestamps), monotonic reads (no time travel: pin a user to a replica), consistent prefix reads (causal order: same-partition writes or explicit causality tracking). Transactions exist so applications needn't hand-roll these.
  • Multi-leader: a leader per datacenter (local writes, survives WAN and datacenter outages), offline clients (every device a leader; calendar sync), collaborative editing. Retrofitted in most databases, riddled with pitfalls, avoided when possible.
  • Write conflicts are detected asynchronously and must resolve convergently: LWW (lossy), replica priority (lossy), merge, or preserve and let the application resolve on write or on read. Avoid conflicts by homing each record on one leader. Automatic resolution: CRDTs, mergeable persistent structures, operational transformation. Some conflicts (double-booked rooms) are invisible to per-record checks.
  • Topologies: circular, star, all-to-all; sparse ones break on one node failure, dense ones let writes overtake each other — a causality problem timestamps can't fix.
  • Leaderless (Dynamo-style): write to all n, wait for w; read from all, wait for r; w + r > n gives overlap. Stale replicas heal by read repair and anti-entropy. No failover. But quorums are probabilistic in practice: sloppy quorums, concurrent writes, partial failures, and restores all leak staleness; you don't get the session guarantees. Sloppy quorums + hinted handoff trade read guarantees for write availability. Monitor staleness even when you tolerate it.
  • Concurrency is about causality, not clocks: A happens-before B if B knows of A; otherwise they're concurrent. LWW forces a fake order and drops data (safe only for immutable keys). The version-number protocol — read before write, send the version, merge what you read, server keeps higher-versioned siblings — loses nothing; removals need tombstones; multiple replicas need version vectors (Riak's dotted version vectors / causal context).
  • 2nd-edition arc: replication's prominence rises relative to sharding; the offline/collaborative thread grows into sync engines, local-first software, and CRDTs (Figma, Linear, Automerge-style tooling); consistency models framed via session guarantees and the baseball paper; version vectors vs vector clocks separated cleanly; Flexible Paxos and newer cloud multi-region systems (Cosmos DB, Aurora DSQL) as examples.

Answer key with explanations
Q1 — c. Kleppmann is explicit: not because shared-nothing is always best, but because it's where the developer must face constraints the database can't hide. (a) is false (the COST paper shows a single thread beating a cluster); (b) and (d) are simply wrong — shared-memory boxes have many CPUs, and shared-disk survives in data warehousing.
Q2 — b. All-synchronous means any node outage halts writes, so systems keep exactly one sync follower (promoting an async one if it fails) — semi-synchronous. (a) is impractical; (c) confuses roles; (d) describes nothing in the chapter.
Q3 — d. The snapshot is a point in time; the follower must replay exactly the changes after that point (PostgreSQL's log sequence number, MySQL's binlog coordinates). No lock is taken (b), and it has nothing to do with checksums or table selection.
Q4 — a. Async lag + failover discarded writes, and the stale counter reused keys — dangerous precisely because another store (Redis) depended on them. (b) is the separate "both nodes shut down" incident; (c) and (d) aren't in the text.
Q5 — c. The WAL is the storage engine's physical log, so a format change between versions makes leader/follower version mismatch impossible — no "upgrade followers, fail over, upgrade leader." Logical logs are the fix, being decoupled from engine internals.
Q6 — b. The user's own write vanished on reload: read-your-writes. Leader reads for self-modified data, time windows after updates, or last-write timestamps are the book's techniques. (a) is the "time goes backward" anomaly; (c) is the causal one; (d) overshoots and breaks read scaling.
Q7 — a. Monotonic reads permits staleness but forbids rewinding; hashing user ID → replica gives it. (b) is strong consistency; (d) is a way to get read-your-writes at the cost of scaling.
Q8 — d. Consistent prefix reads is free when one leader defines one order. Partitions break the single order, so different partitions can be seen at different points in time. Fix: co-locate causally related writes, or track causality explicitly.
Q9 — c. The book's three-way comparison: performance, datacenter-outage tolerance, network-problem tolerance. Multi-leader is worse on consistency, simplicity, and constraints (a) — it introduces conflicts (b) and misbehaves with autoincrements and triggers (d).
Q10 — b. Leader 1 sees B then C, leader 2 sees C then B; naive application diverges permanently. Convergence is the non-negotiable requirement; LWW, replica priority, merging, and preserving conflicts are the ways to get it. (a) would throw away multi-leader's benefit; (c) is one lossy rule, not the reason.
Q11 — d. Node-ID tagging per write; the HBase master/master infinite-loop bug is the cited reference. Timestamps (c) can't be trusted for ordering; TTLs (a) aren't the mechanism.
Q12 — a. The book lists all of these, plus the linearizability timing edge case deferred to Chapter 9. Quorums tune the probability of stale reads; they're not absolute (b). Reads always go to all n (c); anti-entropy (d) is about catching up, not the quorum condition.
Q13 — c. "A sloppy quorum actually isn't a quorum at all in the traditional sense" — writes may land on non-home nodes, so read/write sets needn't overlap. It buys write availability; hinted handoff eventually moves data home.
Q14 — b. Concurrent writes have no true order; LWW invents one from timestamps and drops the losers despite their "ok." Clock skew makes it worse (Chapter 8). Caching tolerates loss; otherwise make keys immutable — Cassandra's UUID-key advice. (d) describes the version-number approach, not LWW.
Q15 — d. The version from the client's prior read says "I've merged everything ≤ v1." So v1 is superseded, v2 is concurrent and kept as a sibling, and the new value gets v3. The client, not the server, does merging (c); rejecting (a) or overwriting all (b) would lose eggs.
